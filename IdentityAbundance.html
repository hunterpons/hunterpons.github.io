The Identity Problem of Abundance Politics

Lessons from Social Identity Theory

The contemporary abundance movement gained significant momentum following the publication of Abundance (2025) by journalists Ezra Klein and Derek Thompson, which synthesized a growing body of work on supply-side progressivism, state capacity, and institutional reform into a coherent political agenda. The central thesis of the book is to maximize the prosperity of all Americans by investing in institutional efficacy and selective deregulation to facilitate faster, cheaper development of housing, public transportation, renewable energy, and healthcare, thereby assuaging the contemporary ills of modern American society. While formalizing these ideas and organizing them under a common moniker has done a great deal to expedite their adoption among intellectuals and politicians alike (nods to Gavin Newsom and Zohran Mamdani), the abundance agenda itself has yet to gain mainstream acceptance.

For the movement to succeed in securing legislative action, it must generate widespread public enthusiasm among Americans from diverse ideological backgrounds. Doing so requires understanding the psychological mechanisms through which people come to identify with the future that abundance proposes. I argue that this identity problem, not merely a policy problem, is one of abundance’s greatest obstacles.

Barrier 1.

Abundance is often communicated as a set of policies rather than a vision of collective identity

Presenting reform as policies to streamline permitting, reduce regulatory burdens, reform zoning procedures, improve agency coordination, and increase housing supply elasticity does little to communicate the end results of those reforms and how they may affect the communities in which they are implemented. People want to know exactly how their lives will change and what risks those policies pose. People are motivated to reduce subjective uncertainty about themselves and the social world by identifying with groups that provide clear norms, values, and behavioral expectations. Consequently, proposals for major social or institutional change may encounter resistance by introducing uncertainty about valued identities or appearing inconsistent with existing group norms. Framing reforms as continuous with core American values may therefore reduce perceived identity threat and facilitate acceptance.

Rampant NIMBYism in liberal cities demonstrates this phenomenon. Strong local resistance to public housing development is at odds with the general support these efforts receive from self-identifying liberals. While supporters of public housing may believe in its necessity, when public housing projects are proposed in their neighborhoods, the threat to their way of life is often met with opposition instead of enthusiasm. Despite purported goodwill toward public housing proposals, support for the initiatives can weaken when the policy becomes local, visible, and personally costly. Public housing, unfortunately, experiences too many regulatory roadblocks initiated by the public to become what it needs to be to have a substantial effect on the issue. Americans must first believe that abundance policies will actually produce the future they promise if they are to make substantive personal sacrifices to achieve it. Leaders who are viewed as highly representative of the group and as legitimate are the only figures capable of earning Americans’ trust that an abundance policy will achieve the future it claims.

Barrier 2.

The movement lacks sufficient identity entrepreneurs

Haslam and colleagues described leaders as “entrepreneurs of social identity,” uniquely positioned to shift group norms through explicit words and implicitly through observation of their behavior. Abundance (2025), by Ezra Klein and Derek Thompson, arguably positioned the authors as the strongest and most coherent advocates in the public media landscape for reducing key regulatory barriers and scarcities in America. While they have carried this mantle admirably by continuing to speak on how the left can overcome assumptions that have contributed to the tenuous economic and political climate we now exist in, they are necessarily limited by their roles as reporters.

Leaders are ultimately required to turn ideas into realities. According to the social identity approach, leadership effectiveness depends on a leader’s ability to mobilize followers toward collective goals by representing and advancing the group’s shared identity. Those leaders who are perceived as prototypical of the American identity, that is, when they are seen as embodying the group’s defining values, norms, and characteristics, exert the greatest influence on their followers, even fundamentally changing the group’s prototype through their behavior. An influential and effective leader can redefine the values and attributes common to all Americans, aligning the tenets of the abundance agenda with features of the American identity.

Barrier 3.

Abundance must be framed as a continuation of American identity

What might redefining the American prototype to more closely align with abundance look like? I once wrote that an abundant American future requires redefining American patriotism, “One that measures national greatness not by military strength or stock market performance but by the quality of life experienced by ordinary citizens. Imagine hearing a president say: ‘When our founders declared independence, they declared our rights to life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness. If millions of Americans cannot afford healthcare, housing, childcare, or have access to quality education and dignified work, then our pursuit remains unfinished.”

Prototypical or representative leaders have incredible latitude to push the prototype to accomplish massive national undertakings. President John F. Kennedy’s famous assertion that America would go to the moon “not because [it is] easy, but because [it is] hard” exemplified identity leadership. Rather than simply arguing that the Apollo program was technologically feasible, Kennedy appealed to an American identity defined by collective achievement, national purpose, and the willingness to undertake difficult challenges. Framed in this way, support for the economic sacrifices associated with the space program became part of what it meant to be American.

Each abundance policy will need to be explicitly tied to pre-existing American values, even if it requires some recontextualization to be compatible with them. A leader might argue that “hard-working Americans deserve quality, affordable childcare because it allows them to remain strong, contributing members of their society. Too long have we relied on Americans to sacrifice their welfare and relationships with their children to achieve success; we envision a world where families can prosper together.” Through extensive use of continuity rhetoric, leaders can recontextualize a vision of America as a nation of inclusive prosperity rather than deep wealth inequality.

Barrier 4.

Avoid identity-threatening intergroup comparisons

A vision of a prosperous America need not be so explicitly tied to derogating the America of today. The abundance agenda does not include novel policies or ideas that have never been implemented before. The inspiration for this movement to maximize American prosperity came from the outcomes of similar policies in other nations, particularly Scandinavia. While it is tempting to cite Scandinavia’s policy outcomes to persuade Americans to adopt similar policies, doing so necessarily makes an intergroup context salient, in which members of the ingroup will attempt to improve their group’s status relative to the outgroup using several strategies described in social identity theory.

This commonly manifests as social creativity, defined as making comparisons with the outgroup along other, more favorable dimensions to increase one’s perceived status relative to that outgroup, which appears superior in a different dimension of comparison. Commonly, skeptics of policies that succeed in other countries will invalidate the relevance of those comparisons by arguing that although such outcomes may have been achieved in a different country, the different circumstances in America would render the same policies ineffective or even detrimental to American society. Of course, this rhetoric is not entirely without merit. Americans frequently argue that America is sufficiently different to negate a legitimate comparison with another country; these arguments often rely on broad assertions about American exceptionalism rather than explicit empirical demonstrations that the policies would function differently under comparable conditions.

Using arguments that elicit intergroup comparisons is likely to be less effective among Americans who rely on their American identity heavily to maintain a positive self-concept. Making these kinds of Americans more aware of how their country is failing creates a dissonant state in which they are forced to defend America and/or invalidate the comparison, or feel shame about America’s inability or unwillingness to implement simple solutions to long-term problems. Proposed solutions to America’s problems are sometimes met with contempt because the possibility of any kind of solution threatens the competence or morality of the American nation, i.e, if there were a way to fix it, we would have done it by now. It is especially threatening for strong party-identifying members when an opposition party espouses policies that might actually improve the country, as it again elicits an intergroup context.

Barrier 5.

Uncertainty increases susceptibility to threat-based political narratives

Uncertainty-identity theory predicts that people become more strongly attracted to groups and leaders that reduce subjective uncertainty by providing clear norms, values, and visions of the future. Political leaders who articulate a coherent narrative about where the nation is headed, therefore, possess a significant persuasive advantage over those who present policies without an accompanying vision of collective identity.

President Donald Trump illustrates this dynamic. Since 2016, he has consistently framed immigration, economic decline, and international conflict as existential threats to the American way of life while simultaneously presenting himself as the leader capable of restoring national strength. Using extensive loss framing, he amplifies uncertainty by presenting followers with an exaggerated, coherent understanding of America’s problems, identifying those responsible, and offering the sole prescription for reducing that uncertainty: a clear vision of national renewal paved by his leadership. Trump’s supporters frequently engage in what Haslam and colleagues describe as engaged followership, acting in ways they believe advance the leader’s goals even without explicit instruction, as seen during the January 6 Capitol riot.

The abundance movement cannot assume that policy proposals alone will satisfy the basic psychological motives of group identification: to reduce uncertainty and achieve a positive social identity. If people are uncertain about the future, as they tend to be in times of economic and political upheaval, they are unlikely to be pacified by abstract technocratic arguments about permitting reform, zoning, or institutional capacity. Every candidate promises they will provide a better future for their constituents than their opponent, but it’s the one that supplies the most coherent and attractive vision of a future America that wins.

Prosperity itself can and should become a central part of that vision. An abundant America should not merely promise greater efficiency or economic growth, but a redefinition of American patriotism in which national greatness is measured by the ability to provide affordable housing, healthcare, childcare, education, and opportunity to ordinary citizens. Not only is it morally imperative to do so, but America is uniquely positioned to solve these challenges, provided it has extensive resources and unmatched determination. The political movement that successfully articulates a compelling “who” (the American identity) and “what” (the institutional reforms) necessary to realize it will be best positioned to secure broad public support.
