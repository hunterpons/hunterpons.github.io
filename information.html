Research Philosophy

I study how social identities shape political perception, intergroup conflict, and collective life. My work draws on the social identity theory framework to understand how people define “us” and “them,” how leaders and movements shape group boundaries, and how political conflict becomes psychologically meaningful.

I am especially interested in polarization, social cohesion, civic trust, and democratic belonging. I approach political division not only as disagreement over issues, but as a process of identity formation in which people interpret opponents, institutions, and social change through the groups they belong to and the groups they define themselves against.

Approach

My aim is to translate social psychological theory into clearer explanations of contemporary political and social conflict. Rather than treating polarization only as misinformation, incivility, or disagreement, I am interested in how identity organizes perception: who people see as similar, who they see as threatening or extreme, and what kinds of messages make common ground more or less possible.

I hope to contribute to applied social research on polarization, framing, narrative, civic trust, and social cohesion by bringing a theoretically grounded perspective on identity, leadership, and group dynamics.
